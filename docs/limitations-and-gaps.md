# Limitations and research gaps

## Limitations

- **Correlation is not causation.** All teen evidence is observational. RAND’s regression is correlational and concerns beliefs and school rules.
- **Self-report.** Use, learning and idea generation are reported by teens, not observed.
- **Sampling.** Pew and RAND use probability-based panels. Common Sense combines probability and nonprobability samples (one-third nonprobability, calibrated by NORC). The NYCPS survey was self-selected and may include duplicates.
- **Cross-sectional.** No source follows the same teens over time. RAND’s waves overlap but were analyzed as separate snapshots, with no significance testing across waves.
- **Beliefs, not abilities.** No audited source measures critical thinking. RAND’s item asks what students believe about students in general.
- **Population mismatch.** RAND covers ages 12–29; its teen-specific high-school figures are withheld pending verification. The experiment is from Turkey.
- **National vs local.** National figures are never used as NYC estimates.
- **No respondent-level data.** No public microdata was found for the U.S. teen surveys, so no inferential statistics were run.
- **No indices.** The project defines no composite scores. The conceptual spectrum and measurement framework are proposals.

## Verification queue

Five evidence points are withheld until confirmed (see `RESEARCH_AUDIT.md`): Pew’s exact daily-use share (category values seen only in news reporting), RAND high-school homework use and critical-thinking belief (read from chart labels), and the NYC June 2026 guidance delay and 2026–27 K–8 moratorium (news reporting only). The Pew January 2025 ChatGPT-schoolwork source is registered but unverified and unused.

## Research gaps

1. **Causal effects on critical thinking in U.S. teens.** No randomized or longitudinal study with a measured critical-thinking outcome.
2. **Long-term effects.** No study follows teens over years of AI use.
3. **AI literacy as a moderator.** Common Sense measures teacher AI instruction and offloading-related items in the same teens, but without public respondent-level data the relationship cannot be tested.
4. **Verification behavior.** How often teens check AI output against other sources is not measured.
5. **Decision reliance.** No measure of teens relying on AI for decisions; the closest is 12% using chatbots for emotional support or advice.
6. **NYC students.** No representative survey of NYC students’ AI use or its effects.

## Future research

Answering these questions needs respondent-level, longitudinal data from teens, collected with parental consent, teen assent and ethics (IRB) review, that measures in the same teens:

- **AI exposure:** frequency, duration, platform, purpose, school vs personal use
- **Cognitive behavior:** attempting tasks before using AI, generating own ideas, verifying and challenging AI output, comparing alternatives
- **Learning:** understanding, retention, confidence, explaining work without AI
- **Decision-making:** use of AI recommendations, independent judgment
- **Context:** grade, age band, school AI rules, AI literacy instruction, access

Two near-term data leads: Pew typically releases case-level teen survey data after a delay (check pewresearch.org/datasets), and RAND Survey Panels may grant access to American Youth Panel data on request.
