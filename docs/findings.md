# Findings

Every figure below is a verified evidence point in `data/evidence.json`, stated with the people who answered. Figures from different surveys are listed separately and must not be compared as a trend.

## 1. AI use is widespread among U.S. teens

*Pew Research Center, U.S. teens 13–17, n=1,458, Sept. 25 – Oct. 9, 2025 (representative).*

- 64% of U.S. teens say they ever use an AI chatbot. 16% use one several times a day or almost constantly.
- ChatGPT is the most used (59% of all teens), followed by Gemini (23%) and Meta AI (20%).
- 51% of parents say their teen uses chatbots, below the 64% of teens who say they do.
- Within the same survey: teens 15–17 use chatbots more than teens 13–14 (68% vs 57%); teens in households earning $75,000+ more than those under $30,000 (66% vs 56%). Daily use is higher among Black (35%) and Hispanic (33%) teens than White teens (22%).

## 2. Information and schoolwork lead

*Pew, all teens.*

- Teens have used chatbots to search for information (57%), get help with schoolwork (54%), have fun (47%), summarize content (42%), create or edit images or videos (38%), get news (19%), have casual conversations (16%) and get emotional support or advice (12%).
- 10% of teens do all or most of their schoolwork with chatbots’ help.

**Different measure, different survey:** Common Sense Media (n=1,017, April–May 2026) finds 70% of teens use any AI tool for schoolwork. This is not a rise from Pew’s 54%; the questions and samples differ.

## 3. Teens report both help and offloading

*Common Sense Media, among teens who use AI for schoolwork (n=665).*

- 63% get an answer from AI in at least one way: 35% then fix or improve it, 31% rewrite it in their own voice, 25% use it as-is.
- 77% use AI without getting a direct answer in at least one way, including brainstorming how to start (45%).
- 66% say AI helps them understand their schoolwork, not just finish it faster; 51% say it makes them a more successful student.
- 39% feel they miss out on learning when they use AI to complete assignments; 38% say they come up with fewer of their own ideas because AI is available.
- Among all teens, 25% first try to work it out themselves when stuck; 10% turn to an AI tool first.

These are self-perceptions. Learning and idea generation were not measured.

## 4. Students believe AI harms critical thinking. That is a belief, not a measurement

*RAND American Youth Panel, enrolled students ages 12–29, n=1,214, Dec. 2025 – Jan. 2026 (not teen-only).*

- 67% agree that more AI use for schoolwork will harm students’ critical thinking; 60% of students who use AI for homework agree, vs 78% of non-users.
- 75% of female and 59% of male students agree.
- In RAND’s own correlational analysis, students who did not think AI harms critical thinking, and whose schools allowed AI, were more likely to use AI for homework.

*Pew:* among teens who expect AI to harm society (n=354), 34% name overreliance or loss of critical thinking or creativity as the main reason.

## 5. Experiments: context, not a verdict

*Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 (contextual).* In a randomized field experiment in one Turkish high school, students who practiced math with an unrestricted GPT-4 tutor got 17% lower grades once access was removed than students without AI. A tutor with learning safeguards largely avoided this. It measures math learning, not critical thinking, and does not describe U.S. teens.

## 6. New York City: policy and public feedback, no student data

*NYC Public Schools public-feedback survey, 6,491 self-selected responses, March 25 – May 8, 2026 (limited).*

- 65.2% of respondents to the NYCPS public-feedback survey named cognitive development (“AI doing the thinking students should do”) as a top concern, including 75.8% of parent and family respondents and 83.1% of community-member respondents.
- 52% of student respondents named mental health and well-being, the group most concerned about it.
- Respondents were mostly educators (47%) and parents or family members (44%); roles could overlap.

These describe survey respondents, not NYC students or parents. No representative survey of NYC students was found.

## 7. The central finding

U.S. teens’ AI use is well measured. What AI does to their thinking is not. No audited source measures a teen’s critical thinking alongside their AI use, follows teens over time, or tests causation in U.S. teens. On the question “does AI reduce critical thinking?”, the answer supported by current public evidence is: **insufficient evidence**.
